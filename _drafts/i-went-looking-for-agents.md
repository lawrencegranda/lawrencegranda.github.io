I went to Cornell's All Industries Career Fair in Barton Hall on a Wednesday afternoon in September. I wanted to hear how people in different industries talk about AI, optimization, and the problems that get in their way. I'm finishing a CS degree and starting an ORIE M.Eng, and I'm done recruiting for full-time. That meant I could walk up and ask what I was curious about, instead of treating every booth like an interview.

I think I walked in with a fairly specific bias. Last summer I spent twelve weeks around production GenAI. The agents generated structured records, and something outside the model had to validate them and keep them in sync with live sources. I tend to look for places where a model, an optimizer, or a workflow agent could take work off people's plates. I assumed I would hear some version of that.

I expected that kind of story. What I actually heard was different. It seemed like the people I was talking to were using chatbots, or at least describing chatbots. They weren't using AI to automate their workflows. You type something, it answers, and you still do the job yourself.

When I say chatbot I mean that box: a question, a reply, maybe a paragraph you paste into a slide. When I say agent I mean something that takes a goal, uses tools for a while, and produces the actual work. That could be a model, a deck, a matched expert, or a filed comment. You still have to review it, but you are not sitting in the loop for every click. I didn't really meet people who spent their weeks working that way.

Once I stopped trying to force an AI story onto every conversation, the conversations fell into a few kinds.

Some shops cannot put the work into a consumer model at all. Edgeworth Economics does litigation economics in antitrust, labor, and IP. The methods they advertise are still regression, SAS, Stata, and R, with Python if you have it. In 2023 they published a piece about LLMs in legal work. The useful part is not that AI will transform expert testimony. Client confidentiality and data security make ChatGPT risky, and that risk applies to expert witnesses, not only to lawyers. An LLM answer is pattern matching, and courts care whether a method is replicable. I didn't need anyone at the booth to spell that out. They had already written it down. The version I heard in the room was simpler: confidential client documents don't go into GPT, and getting the data in the first place is an IT process. That doesn't read to me as a team that failed to adopt agents. If you paste their work into a chatbot, it stops being work they can use.

Some shops have a product that looks like AI and a campus job that doesn't. AlphaSights matches investors and consultants to industry experts. They will tell you about AlphaGraph and AlphaGPT: a knowledge graph, a library of calls, a question box over that library, and automatic summaries of those calls. Campus hiring is still Client Service, with a significant part of the day on the phone. Tech and product are senior hires. Associates get told to use AI sourcing tools, which as far as I can tell are just search. The expert still has to be found, screened, and booked by a person. After the fair I looked at expert-network threads on Reddit, and they sound the same: LinkedIn keywords, then a human. I don't think the company is lying about having a chatbot over its library, and I don't think the person at the booth was lying about the phones. They're describing two different layers of the same firm.

Some shops are mostly in the business of talking about agents for other people. A CEO-advisory firm will hire a Global Head of AI, survey hundreds of CEOs about ROI, and announce a joint venture so a software partner can build the agents. The campus role is still hypotheses and slides. What they named as hard was a lack of clarity, and salespeople discounting too much. I went home and found both of those in their own essays: sense-making as "clarity in complexity," and pricing papers about limiting discounting. Those are real commercial problems. They just aren't a workflow that runs while you sleep.

A healthcare boutique bank didn't have a tech role on the table. The junior job is still Excel and pitch books. What they pointed me at was a vendor, [Rogo](https://rogo.com/), which is probably the actual agent company in this story. It works with company filings, internal documents, Excel, and PowerPoint. It drafts deal memos, financial models, and the research that goes into a deal. They'll tell you every deployment is custom because every banker wants the work done a different way. That sentence, from their own site, felt like the most honest thing I heard all afternoon. The hard part isn't which model you wrap. It's whether the software can be tailored to how people already work. I'm not going to claim that bank runs on Rogo. I am going to claim that when a banker at an All Industries fair wants to talk about AI, they usually don't describe a system they wrote. They describe something you buy, and then spend a long time teaching that tool your house style.

Even there, the public chatter from juniors is mixed in a way I recognize. The basic product often feels like a worse ChatGPT. The agentic product, the one that is supposed to take a goal and go off for a while, is a gated upgrade, and the formatting still comes back wrong. So the one firm in this set that sells agents still seems to deliver a chatbot to most of the people who have a license.

Then there's the booth where generative AI isn't the job because the job is maps, permits, and civil-service titles. The New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection is a GIS shop sitting inside IT. They also have air models and water models that existed long before anyone said Copilot. The state of New Jersey has a generative-AI policy and a list of approved chat assistants. DEP's own genAI, on paper, is a permit-search pilot and a tool to help draft responses to comments. The people hiring at Barton were hiring analyst trainees and IT specialists. If someone at that booth says AI isn't a priority, I think that's true of the jobs they were hiring for. It would be unfair to take it as meaning the agency doesn't model anything. They model plenty. They just aren't hiring you to automate that work with agents.

Two government booths were really about a requirement I couldn't get past: U.S. citizenship. One of them publicly hires software and AI engineers, including rotational development programs. The other commissions officers. Neither conversation was about how someone automated their week. One was eligibility. The other was Officer Candidate Course, the path to becoming a Marine officer. I wrote down the skillset anyway and kept walking.

I looked the firms up afterward, partly because I had gone in under-prepared on purpose. I wanted the unscripted version. Official sites, job ads, a couple of patents, almost nothing useful on Twitter. On Reddit I found threads about the expert network being phone-heavy, and bankers complaining about Rogo. The internet didn't overturn what I heard in the room. It mostly filled in the product names around it.

The people I met seemed to be using chatbots, using no generative AI at all, or recommending a vendor. People were still doing the work themselves.

I don't mean that as a dunk. A lot of the economy is still people, process, and what you're allowed to put in a prompt. I spent a summer on the other side of that line, where the model isn't really the system. The validators and the evals are. From that side it's easy to assume everyone else is just a year behind on agents. I don't think that's always the right read. Some of them aren't behind. They're in a different game. Confidential work can't go in the box. Matching people still requires a call. A state agency already has ArcGIS. A bank would rather buy a copilot than staff an ML team. A consulting firm will sell you a slide about the agentic age and then ask an associate to make the slide.

I still study computer science and operations research, and I still look at messy processes and ask whether technology can make them easier. I'll keep asking. I think I will also stop hearing "we use AI" and assuming it means software that can carry out a multi-step task on its own. A lot of the time, at least this week, it just means a chatbot.

If I go to another fair, I want to try booking a coffee chat before the line forms. The lines at the big firms were long, and a scheduled conversation is probably a better way to hear the same thing more slowly.
